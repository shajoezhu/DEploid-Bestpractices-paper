\subsection{Generating {\it in silico} mixtures for deconvolution benchmark}
\subsection{{\it In silico} mixtures of lab strains}
In addition to the {\it in vitro} lab mixtures from \citet{Wendler2015}, we generated four {\it in silico} lab mixtures. We used the lab strain GB4 to provide a whole-genome read-depth profile, and drew Bernoulli variables to simulate alternative allele counts, with the probability of success set to the adjusted within-sample allele frequency (WSAF) at each position. The expected WSAF is the dot product of the genotype of the lab strains (3D7, Dd2, HB3, 7G8) and the proportions. For example, if the genotype is 0, 0, 1, 1 at an arbitrary position, a mixture proportion of 0.1, 0.2, 0.4 and 0.3 leads to an expected WSAF of 0.7. We then adjust the WSAF with an error term (0.01) to account for technical error/noise: $0.7 \times (1-0.01) + (1-0.7) \times 0.01 = 0.696$.

% \vspace{.5cm}
% To test the accuracy of \texttt{DEploidIBD} in a more realistic setting, we created {\it in silico} mixtures of 2, 3 and 4 strains given different transmission scenarios from mosquito bites. Let $K$ denote the number strains within each sample, and $b$ denote the number of independent mosquito bites. In such a scenario, a mixed infection containing $K$ strains can be treated as a result of $b$-bite events, where $K \in \{2,3,4\}$ and $1~{\leq}~{b}~{\leq}~K$. For example, a $K=2$ mixed infection can result from co-transmission, where two strains are passed in a single bite ($b=1$); or by superinfection, where each strain is delivered by a unique bite ($b=2$). A mixed infection containing 3 strains is more complex: besides co-infection (3 strains in a single bite, $b=1$) and superinfection (3 strains in 3 bites, $b=3$), we can also have a super-infection scenario made up from a co-infection plus a clonal-infection ($b=2$). The complete simulation procedure (code) is available at \url{https://github.com/DEploid-dev/DEploid-Data-Benchmark-in_silico_field}.

\subsection{Data cleaning and filtering}

\subsubsection{High leverage data points}
As in \citet{Zhu2017,Zhu2019}, we find that markers with high frequencies in both reference and alternative allele counts can mislead our model into fitting extra strains. We used a threshold of $\geq$ 99.5\% coverage (default) to identify markers with extremely high allele counts. We further expand this list of potential ``bad markers'' by considering their nearest 10 neighbours on both sides along the genome, and identify the nearest markers as ``to be excluded'' if overlaps are found (see Figure~\ref{fig:fitering}). These poorly-genotyped variants are likely to be artefacts of mapping and genotype calling.

\section{DEploid-LASSO deep dive}
\subsection{Model}
We recall the notation of \citet{Zhu2019}. Using a Bayesian approach, the posterior probability of the strain number $K$, proportions $\mathbf{w}$, haplotypes $\mathbf{h}$ and relatedness state $\mathcal{S}$ given the sequence data $D$ and error rate $e$ is
\begin{equation}
P(K, \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{h}, \mathcal{S} \mid e, D) \propto L(K, \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{h}, \mathcal{S} \mid e, D) \times P(K, \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{h}, \mathcal{S}),
\end{equation}
where the joint prior decomposes as
\begin{equation}
P(K, \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{h}, \mathcal{S}) = P(K) \times P(\mathbf{w} \mid K) \times P(\mathcal{S} \mid K) \times P(\mathbf{h} \mid \mathcal{S}).
\end{equation}
This decomposition allows us to be flexible with the order of inference. In practice we use Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) for inference, and we find that certain execution orders perform better than others, as described in the main text.

\subsection{LASSO reference-panel optimisation}
Let $y$ denote the within-sample allele frequency of the mixed sample. Assuming that the genotypes of each strain in the reference panel $X$ are independent covariates, we fit the linear model
\begin{equation}
y = \beta_0 + \overrightarrow{\beta} X,
\end{equation}
where $\overrightarrow{\beta}$ are the weights of the reference strains that contribute towards the estimated within-sample allele frequency of the mixed genome. A key feature of the LASSO approach is that $\overrightarrow{\beta}$ is sparse, producing zero weights for many reference strains in the panel and thereby reducing its size. Our objective is to find $\overrightarrow{\beta}$ and $\lambda$ such that
\begin{equation}
\min_{\overrightarrow{\beta}} \left\| y - \beta_{0} - \overrightarrow{\beta} X \right\|^{2} + \lambda \left\| \overrightarrow{\beta} \right\|_{1}
\end{equation}
\citep{Friedman2010}.

In practice we search for $\lambda$ between 0 and 1. We start by setting $\lambda = 1$ and $\overrightarrow{\beta} = \mathbf{0}$, then gradually add reference strains into the model by setting individual $\beta$s to non-zero values one at a time. We keep including new reference strains if the new model reduces the likelihood deviance by at least one percent. The LASSO approach lets us discard reference strains with zero $\beta$s, and we further reduce the panel by opting out $\beta$s below 0.01. Note that the choice of the final reference strains depends on the order in which reference strains are included in the computation.

\subsection{Reduced run time}

To isolate the effect of reference-panel size on compute cost, we deconvolved a
fixed set of $l = 18{,}570$ sites (sample PG0390-C) using synthetic reference
panels of $m \in \{10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000\}$ strains, holding the
MCMC settings fixed ($nSample = 20$, $rate = 5$, seed 1, single thread). The
classic {\tt DEploid} and {\tt DEploid-IBD} pipelines scale approximately
quadratically with $m$ and fail to complete within a 5-minute budget once $m$
exceeds roughly 20--50 strains, whereas {\tt DEploid-BEST} completes in roughly
2--3~minutes irrespective of panel size, including $m = 1000$ (Table~S1).

\subsection{Accuracy on in-silico mixtures of known strains}

To quantify inference accuracy we deconvolved four \emph{in-silico} mixtures of
four lab strains (3D7, DD2, HB3, 7G8) with known ground-truth proportions,
including a balanced $0.25/0.25/0.25/0.25$ mixture (Table~S2). Each mixture was
run through the classic {\tt DEploid} pipeline, {\tt DEploid-IBD}, and
{\tt DEploid-BEST} with a four-strain reference panel
($l = 18{,}570$ chromosome-14 sites). {\tt DEploid-BEST} recovered the correct
number of strains ($K = 4$) automatically in every case; the classic pipeline
reached $K = 4$ only because $K$ was supplied as input, while {\tt DEploid-IBD}
merged the two most closely related strain pairs and collapsed to $K = 2$ even
when $K = 4$ was supplied. For proportion recovery, {\tt DEploid-BEST} and the
classic pipeline given the true $K$ are statistically indistinguishable (mean
total absolute error $0.35$ for both; IBD $1.06$). The residual error in both is
dominated by a 3D7/7G8 labelling ambiguity: the two most divergent strains are
exchanged for the largest proportion (e.g.\ for w1 the methods recover
3D7$\approx0.39$, 7G8$\approx0.21$ versus truth $0.1/0.4$), an identifiability
limit of the reference panel rather than a method failure. The practical benefit
of {\tt DEploid-BEST} on these mixtures is therefore its automatic, robust
strain-number selection and its avoidance of the related-strain collapse seen in
{\tt DEploid-IBD}, together with the runtime scaling reported in Table~S1.

\subsection{Accuracy on in-silico mixtures of field strains}

To test the method on realistic data we built \emph{in-silico} mixtures from
genuine \emph{P.\ falciparum} field haplotypes rather than the four reference
lab strains. For each of two field populations (asiaGroup1, africaGroup2) we
sampled a 20-strain reference panel and two further field strains whose
haplotypes were tiled into mosaic ``truth'' genomes with one to four break
points (the 1--4 ``bites'' scenarios); read counts were then simulated at the
observed field coverage by binomial sampling (error rate $0.01$). The true
proportions of the four mosaic components are encoded in the mixture name
(e.g.\ \texttt{p25v25v25v25} denotes a balanced
$0.25/0.25/0.25/0.25$ mix). Because the true strains are \emph{not} members
of the reference panel, the deconvolution cannot be scored by matching
recovered strain proportions; instead we score the recovered \emph{mixture
allele frequency} (WSAF) site by site, correlating the inferred WSAF
($\sum_j h_{sj}\,w_j$ over the recovered haplotypes $h$ and proportions $w$)
against the simulated truth. Each of the 40 mixtures ($2 \times 5 \times 4$)
was deconvolved by the classic pipeline, {\tt DEploid-IBD}, and
{\tt DEploid-BEST} (single seed, $nSample=500$, $rate=8$).

Table~S3 reports the per-site WSAF recovery. All three pipelines recover the
mixture allele frequency with very high accuracy (Pearson $r$ in the range
$0.97$--$0.99$, typically $\approx 0.99$, in every setting): with a dense,
representative field panel the mixture is identifiable regardless of
strain-number misspecification. {\tt DEploid-BEST} attains this accuracy
\emph{without} $K$ being supplied -- the classic and IBD pipelines were both
fixed at the true $K = 4$ -- and its recovery is if anything marginally better
than the fixed-$K$ pipelines in every scenario. {\tt DEploid-BEST}
automatically selects a strain number (mean $K \approx 2.7$ across the 40
mixtures, ranging $2.1$--$3.3$ by scenario, versus the simulated $4$;
Table~S3, last column), under-estimating the true count by merging the most
similar components. Crucially, it does so without $K$ being supplied, and this
under-estimate does not degrade recovery: the mixture allele frequency is still
recovered to $r \approx 0.99$ (Table~S3), matching the fixed-$K = 4$ classic
and IBD pipelines. Together with the lab-strain benchmark (Table~S2) and the
runtime scaling (Table~S1), this shows that on realistic field mixtures
{\tt DEploid-BEST} is as accurate as a correctly specified classical
deconvolution while removing the need to choose $K$ by hand.

\section{Modularised package development}

To facilitate the development and maintenance of the DEploid software, we have modularised the package into several components. Each component is responsible for a specific functionality, making the codebase more organised and easier to manage. The main modules are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{DEploid-Lasso-lib (C++)}: Handles the input and preprocessing of genomic data.
  \item \textbf{DEploid-vcf-lib (C++)}: Contains the core algorithms for deconvolution and inference.
  \item \textbf{DEploid.utils (R)}: Utility functions for data preprocessing.
  \item \textbf{DEploid (C++)}: DEploid core functions, command-line interface.
  \item \textbf{DEploid-r (R)}: An R interface for DEploid, allowing users to interact directly with DEploid results without saving them to text files and reloading them for analysis. Upstream direct dependencies include DEploid and DEploid.utils.
\end{itemize}
